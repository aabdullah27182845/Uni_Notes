## Formal Definition

A CFG is a 4-tuple $G = (V, \Sigma, R, S)$, where
- $V$ refers to non-terminal variables.
- $\Sigma$ refers to the symbols available in the language.
- $R$ is the set of rules, where $R \subseteq V \times (V \cup \Sigma)^*$, or better yet, $A \to w$, where $A \in V$ and $w \in (V \cup\Sigma)^*$ 
- $S$ is the start symbol, $S \in V$, which is a distinguished start symbol.

You can imagine how $R$ works under the example of $G = (\{A,B\}, \{0,1\}, \mathcal{R}, A)$ where $$\mathcal{R}(X) = \begin{cases} &0A1 \text{ if X=A} \\ 
&B \text{ if X=A} \\
&B \text{ if X=}\epsilon
\end{cases}$$And this description of a CFG happens to define the following language $L = \{0^n1^n : n \geq 0\}$.

---
#### Generating Strings using a CFG

1. Set $w$ to be the start symbol.
2. Choose an occurrence of a variable $X$ in $w$ if any, otherwise STOP.
3. Pick a rule where your left hand side is $X$, then replace the chosen occurrence of $X$ in $w$ by the right hand side.
4. Repeat this iterative cycle by going to 2.

We can use this generator to go back to our example earlier: $G = (\{A,B\}, \{0,1\}, \mathcal{R}, A)$
$$
\begin{align*}
A &\Rightarrow 0A1 \\
  &\Rightarrow 00A11 \\
  &\Rightarrow 00B11 \\
  &\Rightarrow 00\epsilon 11 = 0^2 1^2
\end{align*}
$$
Such sequences are called **derivations**.

---
#### Binary Relations over CFGs

Given a CFG, define a binary relation $\xRightarrow{ }$ over $(V\cup \Sigma)^*$ as follows:
	for each $u,v \in (V \cup \Sigma)^*$, for each $A \xrightarrow{} w$ in $\mathcal{R}$, $$uAv \xRightarrow{} uwv$$
We write $\xRightarrow{}^*$ for the reflexive and transitive closure of $\xRightarrow{}$.

The ***language generated by G***, written $L(G)$, is $\{ w \in \Sigma^* : S \xRightarrow{}^*w \}$.

A language is context free if and only if it can be generated by some context free grammar.

---
#### Examples

We can generate the sequences of all palindromes closed under some $\Sigma^*$ by doing the defining a CFG. 

Take $G = (\{S\}, \{a,b,c\}, \mathcal{R}, S)$ where $\mathcal{R}$ is defined with seven rules. $$
S \rightarrow aSa \mid bSb \mid cSc \mid a \mid b \mid c \mid \epsilon
$$
$G$ happens to define every kind of palindrome that can be made by a, b, or c.

We can also generate the sequences of all *well-balanced* parentheses, and we do this by defining a CFG $G = (\{S\}, \{(,)\}, \mathcal{R}, S)$ with our rule set being $$S \xrightarrow{}  (S) \mid SS \mid \epsilon$$And this defines all well balanced parentheses.


**Bigger Example**: We can define a very simple small English language with the following CFG. I will define the rules first since the set of variables and symbols is quite long.
$$
\begin{align*}
\langle \text{SENTENCE} \rangle &\rightarrow \langle \text{NOUN-PHRASE} \rangle \ \langle \text{VERB-PHRASE} \rangle \\
\langle \text{NOUN-PHRASE} \rangle &\rightarrow \langle \text{CMPLX-NOUN} \rangle \mid \langle \text{CMPLX-NOUN} \rangle \ \langle \text{PREP-PHRASE} \rangle \\
\langle \text{VERB-PHRASE} \rangle &\rightarrow \langle \text{CMPLX-VERB} \rangle \mid \langle \text{CMPLX-VERB} \rangle \ \langle \text{PREP-PHRASE} \rangle \\
\langle \text{PREP-PHRASE} \rangle &\rightarrow \langle \text{PREP} \rangle \ \langle \text{CMPLX-NOUN} \rangle \\
\langle \text{CMPLX-NOUN} \rangle &\rightarrow \langle \text{ARTICLE} \rangle \ \langle \text{NOUN} \rangle \\
\langle \text{CMPLX-VERB} \rangle &\rightarrow \langle \text{VERB} \rangle \mid \langle \text{VERB} \rangle \ \langle \text{NOUN-PHRASE} \rangle \\
\langle \text{ARTICLE} \rangle &\rightarrow \text{a} \mid \text{the} \\
\langle \text{NOUN} \rangle &\rightarrow \text{boy} \mid \text{girl} \mid \text{flower} \\
\langle \text{VERB} \rangle &\rightarrow \text{touches} \mid \text{likes} \mid \text{sees} \\
\langle \text{PREP} \rangle &\rightarrow \text{with}
\end{align*}
$$10 variables, 9 terminals, and 18 rules. There is nothing stopping us in a CFG to define a terminal to be longer than a single character long, as long as it's defined well (this is due to the fact the the rule relation maps the variables to the star of the union of terminals and variables).

Here is a derivation of a sentence that can be defined under this context free grammar. $$
\begin{align*}
\langle \text{SENTENCE} \rangle &\Rightarrow \langle \text{NOUN-PHRASE} \rangle \ \langle \text{VERB-PHRASE} \rangle \\
&\Rightarrow \langle \text{CMPLX-NOUN} \rangle \ \langle \text{PREP-PHRASE} \rangle \ \langle \text{VERB-PHRASE} \rangle \\
&\Rightarrow \langle \text{ARTICLE} \rangle \ \langle \text{NOUN} \rangle \ \langle \text{PREP-PHRASE} \rangle \ \langle \text{VERB-PHRASE} \rangle \\
&\Rightarrow \text{a girl} \ \langle \text{PREP} \rangle \ \langle \text{CMPLX-NOUN} \rangle \ \langle \text{VERB-PHRASE} \rangle \\
&\Rightarrow \text{a girl with} \ \langle \text{CMPLX-NOUN} \rangle \ \langle \text{VERB-PHRASE} \rangle \\
&\Rightarrow \text{a girl with} \ \langle \text{ARTICLE} \rangle \ \langle \text{NOUN} \rangle \ \langle \text{VERB-PHRASE} \rangle \\
&\Rightarrow \text{a girl with a flower} \ \langle \text{VERB-PHRASE} \rangle \\
&\Rightarrow \text{a girl with a flower} \ \langle \text{CMPLX-VERB} \rangle \\
&\Rightarrow \text{a girl with a flower} \ \langle \text{VERB} \rangle \ \langle \text{NOUN-PHRASE} \rangle \\
&\Rightarrow \text{a girl with a flower likes} \ \langle \text{CMPLX-NOUN} \rangle \\
&\Rightarrow \text{a girl with a flower likes} \ \langle \text{ARTICLE} \rangle \ \langle \text{NOUN} \rangle \\
&\Rightarrow \text{a girl with a flower likes the boy}
\end{align*}
$$
